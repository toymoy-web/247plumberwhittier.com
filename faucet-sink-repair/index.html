The Small Leaks That Cost The Most

A faucet drip looks harmless, but it adds up. A fixture dripping once per second can waste thousands of gallons a year, and a hidden leak under a sink can warp a cabinet and grow mold. Faucet and sink repair in Whittier fixes both before they escalate.

Hard water adds to the wear. Scale builds up in aerators, cartridges, and valve seats, and Whittier's water comes from several providers whose hardness varies. A fixture that lasted years in one home may wear out sooner in another for that reason alone.

Common Faucet And Sink Problems

A steady drip from the spout

A worn cartridge, washer, or valve seat is usually the cause.

Low or uneven flow

A clogged aerator or scaled cartridge restricts the stream.

Water under the sink

A loose supply line, a worn trap connection, or a leaking faucet base.

A handle that is stiff or loose

Worn internal parts or a loose set screw.

What To Do If Water Is Under The Sink

Act quickly, because a wet cabinet floor swells and can grow mold within a day or two.

  • Close the two small shutoff valves on the supply lines under the sink, turning them clockwise.
  • If a valve will not turn or the leak continues, close the main water shutoff for the house.
  • Empty the cabinet, set a bucket under the leak, and mop up standing water.
  • Run a dry paper towel along each connection to find the wet spot, and photograph it.
  • Do not tighten a plastic nut with a wrench. Overtightening cracks it. Call us to replace the part.

Under-Sink Parts That Fail

Water under a sink often comes from a supply part and not the faucet itself.

Supply lines

Braided lines can corrode at the fittings or burst when they are old.

Angle stop valves

Shutoff valves can seize or leak at the stem after years of sitting.

P-trap and slip joints

Loose nuts or worn washers leak slowly onto the cabinet floor.

Sprayer hose

Kitchen sprayer hoses can crack or leak at the connection under the sink.

Lead-Free Rules And Fixture Choices

Faucets sold in California must meet the state's lead-free standard, which limits lead content in the wetted surfaces. If an older faucet is being replaced, a modern fixture also usually flows at a lower rate and saves water.

Choose a fixture that has replaceable cartridges and available parts. A cheap faucet with a sealed cartridge can cost more over time than a better one that can be rebuilt.

Look for faucets that meet the ASME A112.18.1 and CSA B125.1 standard for plumbing supply fittings, along with NSF/ANSI 61 and NSF/ANSI 372 for drinking water contact and lead content. Install and parts selection should also follow the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

Flow Rates, Aerators, And Water-Saving Rules

California treats interior faucets that use more than 2.2 gallons per minute as noncompliant in buildings built before 1994, and sellers must disclose them. A new aerator or faucet rated at or under 2.2 gallons per minute brings a fixture into line.

Aerators also collect mineral scale, which is a common reason for weak flow. Unscrewing and cleaning one takes a few minutes, and a replacement costs little.

Choosing A Replacement Faucet

Start with the number of holes in the sink or counter, because a faucet must match the mounting layout. Then check the finish, the spout height, and whether a pull-down sprayer will clear the sink bowl.

Look for a faucet that meets the NSF/ANSI 61 lead standard for drinking water contact and carries a flow rating of 2.2 gallons per minute or less. A faucet that fits those two tests is easier to install and to sell later.

Measure the supply line length and check whether the existing shutoff valves still turn. If a valve is seized or weeping, replace it during the same visit, because it is the part you will want working the next time something leaks. A shutoff that turns freely today is worth far more than one that fails during a leak.
