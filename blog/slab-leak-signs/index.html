Slab Leak Warning Signs: What To Check And Do First

A leak under a concrete floor rarely announces itself. These signs, and the lookalikes that fool people, can save you weeks of guessing.

The Short Answer

The four most reliable signs are a water meter that moves with everything off, a warm patch on the floor, the sound of running water in a quiet house, and damp carpet or baseboards with no visible source. Any one deserves a meter test, and two or more together make a slab leak likely.

A slab leak is a break in a water supply line that runs in or under the concrete floor. It is not a crack in the concrete itself, though water can travel along cracks and surface far from the source.

The Warning Signs

A spinning meter

With every fixture off, a moving indicator on the water meter means water is escaping somewhere.

A warm spot on the floor

A leaking hot water line can warm the concrete above it. Walk barefoot across tile or concrete and note any patch that stays warm.

Running water sounds

A faint hiss or trickle when the house is silent suggests pressurized water escaping beneath the floor.

Moisture with no source

Damp carpet edges, lifting laminate, musty smells, or stained baseboards without a plumbing fixture nearby.

A jump in the water or gas bill

A hidden hot water leak makes the heater run more often, so energy use climbs along with water use.

Lookalikes That Fool People

Not every warm floor is a leak. Some older homes have heating ducts running in or under the slab, and those can warm the floor above them when the furnace runs. A sunny window can also warm a patch of tile for hours.

The clue is timing. Heat from ducts tracks the furnace schedule and fades when it shuts off. Heat from a hot water leak stays warm all day and is warmest right after hot water has been used. Moisture on a slab can also come from groundwater or a plumbing drain leak, which is why a meter test comes first.

What To Do In The First Hour

Acting quickly keeps repair costs down and protects floors and walls.

  • Do the meter test: turn off all water, note the reading, and watch the leak indicator.
  • Close the main shutoff at the house and check the meter again. If the indicator stops, the leak is on your side of the main.
  • Mark any warm or damp spots with painter's tape, and photograph them.
  • Check the water heater, the toilets, and the sink supply lines to rule out easier explanations.
  • Call for leak detection. Do not break concrete yourself, because pipes, gas lines, and electrical conduit may sit nearby.

Why Pipes Under Slabs Fail

Slab leaks usually come from long-term wear. Copper lines under concrete can develop pinholes from corrosion or from rubbing against rock and rebar as the pipe expands and contracts. Water chemistry and high pressure speed the process, and soil movement can stress joints.

Many Southern California homes from the 1950s have copper supply lines in or under the slab. After seven decades, the pipes in some of those homes are simply old. Whittier has both slab and raised-foundation houses, so the first question when you call is which one you have.

If the test and the signs point under the floor, our page on slab leak detection and repair explains how leaks are located and fixed, including reroutes that avoid breaking the slab. For leaks that could be elsewhere, hidden leak detection narrows the search. If several leaks have appeared, pricing home repipe service may beat repairing each one.

What Repair Looks Like Once It Is Confirmed

A confirmed slab leak usually leads to one of three paths. A spot repair opens the slab over the leak and fixes the pipe, a reroute abandons the leaking line and runs new pipe overhead through walls or the attic, and a repipe replaces the supply system.

The right path depends on the pipe's condition, where it runs, and how many leaks have already appeared.

Repairs and new pipe should follow the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Ask the technician to show you the pressure test or listening results before approving anything, and ask what happens to the flooring over the repair. Restoring tile, wood, or carpet is often a separate line item from the plumbing.

How Detection Confirms A Slab Leak

If your own checks point to a leak under the floor, a technician usually confirms it in a set order. Each step rules out part of the house before anything is opened.

Meter test

Proves water is moving when it should not be.

Zone isolation

Hot and cold lines are closed off in turn to learn which one is losing water.

Listening

Sensitive microphones mark the loudest point on the floor.

Confirmation

A pressure test or tracer gas check proves the exact spot before any concrete is cut.
