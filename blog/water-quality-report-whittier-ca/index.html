How to Read Your Water Quality Report in Whittier, CA

The annual report your water provider sends is the best free source on your tap water. Here is how to find the numbers that matter.

By the Whittier 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 2, 2026 · Updated October 12, 2026

The Short Answer

Your water provider must send you an annual water quality report, often called a Consumer Confidence Report. It names your water source, lists the contaminants found and at what levels, and compares them with the legal limits. Look for the hardness number, which is usually listed in milligrams per liter, and divide by 17.1 to get grains per gallon.

Hardness is not a health issue, but it decides how fast scale builds up in your water heater and fixtures. It is also the number a softener installer needs to size a system.

Why The Report Exists And When It Arrives

Under the Safe Drinking Water Act, administered mainly by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, water suppliers must send customers information about drinking water quality every year. The reports are due to customers by July 1 and cover the previous calendar year. They describe where the water comes from, what contaminants are present, and at what levels.

Many providers now post the report online and mail only a notice. If you cannot find yours, call the provider and ask for the latest edition.

Find Your Provider And Your Report

Whittier is served by several providers, so start by confirming which one serves your address.

  • Check your water bill. It names the provider, which may be City of Whittier Water, Suburban Water Systems, San Gabriel Valley Water Company, Orchard Dale Water District, or another utility.
  • Search the provider's website for 'water quality report' or 'consumer confidence report' and choose the most recent year.
  • If your home sits in a system that buys water from others, read the section that lists each source. Some systems blend groundwater with imported water.
  • Download the report and keep it with your home records. Compare it year to year for changes.

Hardness: Not Regulated, But Useful

The EPA has not set a legal limit for hardness, because the calcium and magnesium that cause it are not toxic. The U.S. Geological Survey uses four bands, measured as calcium carbonate: soft at 0 to 60 mg/L, moderately hard at 61 to 120, hard at 121 to 180, and very hard above 180.

In grains per gallon, those bands run roughly 0 to 3.5, 3.5 to 7, 7 to 10.5, and over 10.5. One grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L, so a report that lists 171 mg/L means 10 grains per gallon.

Sections You Will Find In The Report

Layouts differ by provider, but most reports follow the same outline.

Source water

Names the wells, reservoirs, or wholesalers that supply your system and may include a source water assessment.

Detected contaminants

Lists each regulated substance found, the level detected, the legal limit, and the likely source.

Violations and notices

States whether the system met all standards during the year and explains any exceedance.

Health information

Includes required language on lead and other topics for sensitive groups.

Questions To Ask Your Provider

If something in the report is unclear, a call to the provider is free and quick.

  • What is the hardness of the water at my address, and does it vary by season?
  • Which of the system's sources serves my street at the moment?
  • Does the provider offer free or low-cost lead testing or service line information?
  • Is there a backflow program for homes with irrigation?
  • Where do I report taste, color, or odor problems?

What To Do With The Numbers

A hard or very hard reading explains white crust on faucets, spotty glassware, and a rumbling water heater. It also affects maintenance. Tankless heater makers commonly recommend annual flushing, and hard water areas may need it about every six months.

If you want to treat the water, our page on water softener installation and repair covers sizing from your reading. If your goal is taste or a specific contaminant, water filtration service explains how to match a filter to the report. Owners of tankless heaters can use the number to set a flush schedule.

In La Mirada, Suburban Water Systems operates the local system and blends groundwater from the Main San Gabriel and Central basins with purchased supply, so its report is the one to read there.

What The Report Cannot Tell You

A water quality report describes water as it leaves the treatment plant or wells and enters the distribution system. It cannot see your home's plumbing. Lead, for example, can enter water from old service lines, solder, or fixtures inside a building even when the system meets its standards.

If your home was built long before modern lead limits, or you notice rusty or discolored water, a lab test of your own tap tells you more than the report can. Use a state-certified laboratory and follow its sampling instructions.

Whittier Reports Differ By Provider

The City of Whittier's 2024 water quality report says the City pumped 100 percent of its source water from six active deep wells, drawing on the Main San Gabriel and Central groundwater basins. The same report lists total hardness at an average of 250 mg/L, with a range of 230 to 270 mg/L.

That number applies to City of Whittier water. Suburban Water Systems serves portions of Whittier, La Habra, and La Habra Heights from its own system and publishes its own report, so a neighbor may be on a different provider. The EPA says systems must deliver the report to customers by July 1 each year. Households in La Mirada and La Habra should read the report for their own provider.
