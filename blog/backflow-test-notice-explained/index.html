Got A Backflow Test Notice? What It Means And What To Do

A letter asking for a backflow test usually means a device on your property protects the public water supply and is due for its yearly check.

By the Whittier 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 2, 2026 · Updated October 2, 2026

The Short Answer

A backflow test notice from your water provider means a backflow prevention device on your property must be tested and the results sent to the provider by a deadline. Find the device, read the deadline on the notice, hire a tester your provider accepts, and make sure the passing report reaches the provider on time.

Do not ignore it. Providers can follow up with reminders and penalties, and California's cross-connection rules allow suppliers to take action on connections that are not protected. Details depend on your provider, so call the number on the notice if anything is unclear.

Why Providers Send Them

Backflow is water flowing the wrong way. A drop in pressure in the main can pull water from a sprinkler line, a boiler, or a fire system back toward the public supply, bringing fertilizer, chemicals, or stagnant water with it. A backflow assembly is the mechanical check that prevents this.

California's cross-connection control rules are in Title 17 of the California Code of Regulations. They direct water suppliers to protect the public system, and they call for testing of assemblies on a regular schedule, commonly yearly. Each supplier runs its own program, with its own list of testers, forms, and deadlines.

The assemblies themselves are built to published standards such as ASSE 1013 and ASSE 1015. Installation follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

What To Do Before The Tester Arrives

A little preparation avoids a second trip.

  • Locate the device. Irrigation devices are often in a green box or against the house, and fire line devices may be in a vault or a room.
  • Clear plants, debris, and obstructions so the tester has access to the test ports.
  • Turn off your irrigation controller for the day, since water to the system may be shut off briefly.
  • Gather the notice, your account number, and any past test reports.
  • Ask the tester who will submit the results, and ask for a copy for your records.

What Happens After The Test

Pass

Results are recorded and filed with the provider.

Fail

The device is repaired or replaced, then retested.

File

The passing report reaches the provider before the deadline.

Repeat

Mark your calendar for next year's test.

Signs A Device May Fail

Water spitting from a relief port

A reduced pressure assembly that dumps water can have a fouled check valve.

Dripping test cocks

Leaking test ports point to worn seals.

Cracked or damaged body

Impact or freezing can crack a bonnet or body.

A missed prior test

A skipped year is the most common reason a provider escalates.

Home Devices Versus Business Devices

The kind of device on your property usually follows the hazard it protects against.

Typical home device

  • Often a vacuum breaker on an irrigation system.
  • Installed above the highest sprinkler.
  • Simple to reach and test.
  • Needs an annual test if your provider requires it.

Typical business device

  • Often a reduced pressure or double check assembly.
  • Protects boilers, fire lines, and process water.
  • May sit in a vault or a utility room.
  • Often has stricter reporting rules.

How Providers Differ

Whittier-area water providers each run their own cross-connection program. One may use an online portal where testers upload results, another may mail paper forms, and a third may send reminders by email before escalating. The deadline, the list of accepted testers, and the filing method are all set by the provider, so a neighbor served by a different utility may follow different steps.

That is why the notice itself is your most important document. Read the whole letter, call the number on it if any step is unclear, and keep a record of the date you filed.

If You Rent Or Manage A Property

Tenants who receive a notice should pass it to the landlord or property manager right away, since the device usually belongs to the property owner. Managers of multi-tenant buildings often have several devices, and each may have its own deadline.

Keep a simple spreadsheet of every device, its location, its test date, and the provider's due date. A schedule like that turns an annual scramble into a routine, and it gives a new manager or owner a clear record of what has been done. Review it every quarter so no date sneaks up on you.

Common Mistakes With Backflow Notices

Waiting until the last week

Testers book up before deadlines, and a failed device needs time for repair and retest.

Hiring an unapproved tester

A test the provider will not accept has to be paid for twice.

Assuming the tester files the result

Confirm who submits the report, and keep a copy.

Burying the device in landscaping

Plants and mulch that block the test ports slow the visit and can raise the cost.
