What Are Your Pipes Made Of? A Guide For 1950s Homes

Knowing your pipe material tells you what can go wrong and when. Most tests take a magnet, a coin, and a flashlight.

By the Whittier 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 2, 2026 · Updated October 2, 2026

The Short Answer

Look at the pipe where it enters your water heater, under a sink, or near the main shutoff. Reddish-brown metal is copper, and dull gray metal with threaded joints is probably galvanized steel that a magnet will stick to.

Flexible red, blue, or white tubing is PEX, and cream or pale yellow rigid plastic is CPVC. Black or gray heavy pipe in the drain line is usually cast iron or plastic.

Supply pipes bring water in, and drain pipes carry it out. They are different materials, and they fail in different ways. This guide covers both.

How To Test A Pipe Safely

Do these tests on exposed pipe only, and never cut or heat a pipe to identify it.

  • Use a flashlight and check under sinks, near the water heater, and at the main shutoff for exposed pipe.
  • Hold a magnet to the pipe. If it sticks firmly, the pipe is steel, and galvanized if it is dull gray.
  • Gently scratch a small spot with a coin or key. Copper shows a bright reddish color, and galvanized shows silver-gray.
  • Look for markings printed on plastic pipe, such as PEX, CPVC, or PVC, along with a size and a rating.
  • Photograph what you find, including any labels, so you can show a plumber or inspector.

Why 1950s Homes Matter

The 1950s is Whittier's most common build decade, and homes from that era contain a mix. Many have original copper or galvanized steel supply lines, cast iron or clay drains, and later repairs in plastic. Seven decades is long for any metal pipe, so it is worth knowing which type you have.

A single repaired section can be a different material from the rest. A plumber who replaced a leaking section in the 1990s may have joined copper to galvanized, and mixed metals corrode at the joint. That is a good reason to check more than one spot.

Where To Look In The House

Exposed pipe is easiest to read in these spots.

  • At the water heater, where cold and hot lines connect and the pipe is usually visible.
  • Under kitchen and bathroom sinks, on the supply lines and on the drain trap.
  • At the main shutoff valve, often on an outside wall or in the garage.
  • In a crawlspace or garage, where pipe often runs along the framing.
  • At the sewer cleanout outside, where the end of the drain pipe is exposed.

Pipes To Ask A Professional About

A few finds deserve a professional opinion before you do anything else.

Dull gray, soft metal

Soft metal that scratches bright silver can be lead. Lead plumbing was banned for drinking water in 1986, but old fittings can remain. Get the water tested.

Gray flexible plastic

Polybutylene was used from about 1978 to 1995, which postdates most 1950s builds but can appear in remodels. It has a reputation for failing.

Green crusts or white flaking

Corrosion at joints points to slow leaks or incompatible metals.

Rusty hot water

Orange or brown water often means steel pipe or a water heater tank is rusting.

What A Technician Will Ask And Do

When you call, expect questions about the age of the house, past repairs, and where you saw each material. Photos help the technician decide what to bring and whether the visit needs a camera or a pressure test.

On site, the technician will document what the pipes are made of, test for leaks and pressure, and explain the repair options and their cost before any work starts. New or replacement pipe follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and drinking water pipe should carry NSF/ANSI 61 certification.

What To Do With What You Learn

Record

Write down each material and where you saw it.

Compare

Match it with known age and failure patterns.

Test

Run a meter test and watch for leaks and pressure drops.

Plan

Decide on repairs or repiping before a failure forces the choice.

Where To Go Next

If your lines are galvanized steel or aging copper with repeated leaks, whole-home repiping is worth pricing. If you suspect a hidden leak, electronic leak tracing finds it without opening walls. For clay or cast iron drains with roots, a camera visit is the first step toward sewer spot repair.

La Mirada was built out quickly in the 1950s, so many La Mirada houses share the same era and the same pipe types. If one house on your street has had repairs, the others probably have the same pipes. Ask neighbors what their plumbers found, since a shared history is a useful clue.
