Water Bill Climbing And No Visible Leak?

A water bill that jumps with no change in habits, a meter that keeps turning when every tap is off, or a patch of damp drywall with no pipe in sight all point to a leak you cannot see. Leak detection in Whittier finds the exact spot before anyone cuts into a wall.

Emergency Leak Detection: What To Do Before We Arrive

A leak becomes an emergency when water is reaching a ceiling, a floor, or electrical equipment, or when the meter keeps moving with every fixture off. Call the 24/7 line, and use these steps to limit the damage while a technician is on the way.

Find the main shutoff

Turn the main valve clockwise until it stops. If the meter still moves with the valve closed, the leak is usually on the supply line before the valve.

Keep power away from water

If water is near outlets, a panel, or a ceiling light, leave that circuit off and stay clear until it is dry or checked.

Photograph the damage

Take photos of stains, wet areas, and the meter reading. They help the technician plan the first test and support an insurance claim.

Describe what you see

Tell the dispatcher where the water shows, how fast the meter moves, and whether the house sits on a slab or a raised floor.

How Hidden Leaks Get Found

Detection works by narrowing the search. A technician first confirms that water is escaping, then isolates which part of the system is losing it, and only then listens or scans for the exact point. Each stage rules out a large share of the house.

Methods commonly used include acoustic listening devices that hear the hiss of pressurized water, thermal imaging that shows temperature changes behind a surface, and pressure testing of isolated lines. For leaks that stay quiet, a non-toxic tracer gas can be pushed into the line and sniffed out at the surface.

Ask the dispatcher which method suits your situation. Any repaired line is pressure tested afterward, as the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), calls for.

Whittier's housing adds variety to the search. The 1950s is the city's most common build decade, and homes of that age can hide supply lines in walls, in crawlspaces, or beneath concrete. Knowing the foundation type shapes where the search starts.

Test Your Own Water Meter First

This takes about ten minutes and tells you whether water is leaking at all. Your water provider's meter is usually in a box near the curb.

  • Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker and any irrigation timer.
  • Open the meter box and find the leak indicator, a small triangle or dial that spins when any water flows.
  • If it moves with everything off, water is leaking somewhere between the meter and the house or inside the house.
  • Close the main shutoff at the house and check again. If the indicator stops, the leak is inside. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the line between the meter and the house.
  • Write down the meter reading, wait an hour with no water use, and read it again. A change confirms it.

When The Water Bill Is The First Sign

A bill that jumps in one cycle and a bill that creeps up month after month point to different problems. A sudden jump often traces to a running toilet, a stuck irrigation valve, or a pipe that has just failed. A slow climb tends to mean a small leak that has been running for a long time.

Many water providers send a leak alert or can show usage by the day or hour. An alert says that water is flowing constantly, but it does not say where. Ask your provider what it can show, then use the meter test above to learn whether the leak is inside the house or out by the street.

Check the unit on your bill before comparing months, since providers bill in different units. If the number moved and your habits did not, the call is worth making before the next cycle posts.

Leaks In Ceilings, Walls, Garages, And Under The House

Water rarely shows up where it starts. These are the patterns that come up most often.

Ceiling stain below a bathroom

Water follows framing and drips from the lowest point. A stain in the dining room can start at a tub drain, a shower valve, or a supply line in the room above.

Bubbling paint or damp drywall

A wall that feels cool or soft often has a pinhole in a supply line behind it. The wet patch may sit several feet from the actual leak.

Garage wall or floor

Supply lines to a water heater, a laundry hookup, or a garage sink can leak behind a wall where nobody looks. A damp edge where the slab meets the wall deserves a meter test.

Raised floor or crawlspace

Homes on a raised foundation can hold a leak under the floor for months. Soft flooring, a musty smell, or a floor that feels cold and damp are the clues.

A wet spot far from the source

Water can travel along a pipe or under a slab before it surfaces. Where it appears may be far from where it starts.

Yard, Irrigation, Pool, And Spa Leaks

Outdoor leaks hide in plain sight. A patch of lush green grass or soggy soil above a buried line, an irrigation valve that runs faster than it used to, or a wet spot near the meter box all point to a pressurized line losing water. Irrigation lines and the supply to a spa or pool are common culprits because they run underground and shift with the soil.

A leak that the lawn hides can still drive up the bill. If a pool or spa needs frequent refilling, or a sprinkler zone runs weak, say so when you call. It helps the technician decide where to begin.

Rentals, Multifamily Buildings, And Homes For Sale

Owners of a duplex, a triplex, or a four-unit building often cannot tell which unit is losing water, because a shared meter hides it. The same meter test works, and each unit's supply can then be isolated one at a time. Property managers can call for the building and give the unit numbers up front.

Sellers and buyers call when an inspection or a bill turns up a suspect. A leak found before closing is far easier to settle than one found after. For shopping plazas and larger properties, our commercial plumbing page covers business leak calls.

Detect First Or Open The Wall

Opening a wall on a guess costs money and creates a second repair. Pinpointing the leak first usually shrinks both.

Exploratory demolition

  • Fast to start.
  • Often opens more wall than the leak needs.
  • Adds patching and paint to the bill.
  • May miss the leak on the first cut.

Detection before repair

  • Marks the exact spot.
  • Limits the opening to a small patch.
  • Gives an insurer a clear source of loss.
  • Costs a diagnostic fee before the repair.

What A Leak Report Gives You

A good detection visit ends with more than a spot on the floor. You should leave with the location, the likely cause, and a repair path that opens as little of the house as possible.

Keep the findings and photos. If water damage reached walls or floors, written documentation of where the leak was and when it was found helps when you talk to your insurer.
